# v0.17.0 Product Expert Foundation Plan

## Release Goal

`v0.17.0` introduces the Product Expert as the sixth operational expert. The Code Assistant currently has implementation correctness through the GAUSS Language Expert, methodological correctness through the Econometrics Expert, maintainability/design correctness through the Software Engineering Expert, documentation/explanation correctness through the Documentation Expert, and education/learning-design correctness through the Education Expert. None of the five owns product reasoning as such: whether a feature idea actually solves a real user problem, fits a user's workflow, has a credible path to adoption, and is worth prioritizing against alternatives — independent of whether the underlying code is syntactically valid, the underlying method is methodologically sound, the underlying design is maintainable, a single piece of content is clearly documented, or a learning experience is well-sequenced. The Product Expert closes that gap:

- **GAUSS Language Expert** — implementation correctness.
- **Econometrics Expert** — methodological correctness.
- **Software Engineering Expert** — maintainability/design correctness.
- **Documentation Expert** — documentation/explanation correctness.
- **Education Expert** — education/learning-design correctness.
- **Product Expert** — user problem framing, workflow fit, adoption, value proposition, positioning, prioritization, and product tradeoff correctness.

This remains documentation/evaluation-driven expert routing, exactly like the five experts before it: an operational profile, activation triggers, decision rules, and manually-evaluated benchmarks — not a runtime orchestration engine. No code parses or enforces any of this at runtime; a response is checked against it manually, the same way task-mode fit is checked today. Adding a sixth expert changes the *shape* of routing (more combinations to document and evaluate) but not its *mechanism*.

## Expert Boundary Rules

- GAUSS-specific syntax claims must defer to the GAUSS Language Expert and verified evidence — the Product Expert never asserts exact GAUSS syntax on its own authority.
- Econometric/statistical claims must defer to the Econometrics Expert — the Product Expert never asserts model appropriateness or causal validity on its own authority.
- Code design/refactoring claims must defer to the Software Engineering Expert — the Product Expert never asserts maintainability on its own authority.
- Documentation/reference clarity claims must defer to the Documentation Expert — the Product Expert never asserts reference-page quality as its own primary authority.
- Learning-design claims must defer to the Education Expert — the Product Expert never asserts instructional sequencing or pedagogy as its own primary authority.
- The Product Expert may frame user problems, product tradeoffs, adoption risks, and value propositions but cannot invent GAUSS syntax.
- The Product Expert may reason about whether an analysis feature seems useful to a user but cannot certify statistical validity.
- The Product Expert may reason about maintainability as a product risk (e.g., technical debt slowing future delivery) but cannot certify code design.
- The Product Expert may recommend documentation or education as adoption supports but cannot certify documentation or learning quality — those remain the Documentation Expert's and Education Expert's claims respectively.
- The Product Expert may identify assumptions and hypotheses but cannot claim market proof without evidence — a plausible-sounding user story is not validated demand.
- Good product positioning does not prove implementation correctness.
- Strong user demand does not prove statistical validity.
- A useful feature idea does not prove maintainability.
- Good training or documentation does not prove product value.
- The Product Expert is distinct from the Education Expert:
  - **Education** supports learner development — building durable understanding and skill in users who already need the product.
  - **Product** supports user problem framing, workflow fit, adoption, positioning, and prioritization — whether the product itself is worth building, teaching, or documenting in the first place.
  - Good training or documentation can support adoption but never proves a feature is valuable or should be prioritized (the inverse of the boundary `v0.16.0`'s Education Expert already established from its own side).
- The Product Expert is distinct from the Documentation Expert:
  - **Documentation** helps a user complete a task or understand a reference right now.
  - **Product** frames whether the underlying feature solves a real problem and is positioned/prioritized correctly.
  - Strong positioning or messaging does not eliminate the need for clear documentation, and clear documentation does not itself constitute positioning or a value proposition.
- If multiple experts activate, implementation, methodology, maintainability, documentation, education, and product claims must remain separate and separately attributable in the response, extending the same coordination principle `v0.9.0`, `v0.14.0`, `v0.15.0`, and `v0.16.0` established for two, three, four, and five experts to six.

## Multi-Expert Routing Impact

Current routing axes (`v0.9.0`-`v0.16.0`, five experts): GAUSS implementation, econometric methodology, software-engineering/code quality, documentation/explanation quality, education/learning-design quality — expressed through a compact activation matrix and common worked patterns rather than a full combinatorial enumeration.

`v0.17.0` adds a sixth axis:

1. GAUSS implementation
2. Econometric methodology
3. Software engineering/code quality
4. Documentation/explanation quality
5. Education/learning-design quality
6. Product/user-value quality

A fully enumerated six-expert combination table would have 64 rows (2⁶). This plan explicitly does **not** propose enumerating all 64. Instead, `workflows/expert-routing.md`'s eventual six-expert update should:

- Keep the existing routing-pattern approach `v0.15.0` established and `v0.16.0` extended (compact activation matrix plus worked examples for the combinations most likely in practice, with a general coordination rule covering any combination without its own named row).
- Add Product as a sixth column in the activation matrix.
- Keep explicit, worked examples for the Product-involving combinations most likely to occur in practice: Product-only, GAUSS + Product, Econometrics + Product, Software Engineering + Product, Documentation + Product, Education + Product, Documentation + Education + Product, GAUSS + Documentation + Education + Product, GAUSS + Econometrics + Product, all six, and Neither — the combinations named in this plan's Benchmark Plan and routing-examples list below.
- Preserve every existing five-expert pattern already documented in `v0.16.0` unchanged, adding Product only where a worked example specifically needs it, rather than rewriting the whole matrix from scratch.
- Preserve Neither-expert routing unchanged for ordinary writing tasks unrelated to product, technical education, documentation, code, GAUSS, or econometrics.
- Ensure the Product Expert can combine with any subset of the other five experts, including all six at once, governed by the same coordination principles (claims stay separable; the strictest applicable evidence/context requirement wins) already established for the five-expert case.
- This is the same "readable at N combinations, not a combinatorial engine" discipline `v0.14.0` committed to at eight, `v0.15.0` extended to twelve, and `v0.16.0` extended to fourteen; this plan extends that discipline further rather than abandoning it at sixty-four.

### reasoning.md

Should describe a step-by-step reasoning workflow, following the `experts/education/reasoning.md` precedent (a numbered workflow plus special-case sections):

1. Identify the user/customer and the problem.
2. Identify the workflow and use case.
3. Identify the desired outcome or value.
4. Identify adoption/time-to-value risks.
5. Separate product value from implementation correctness — a compelling feature story is never evidence the GAUSS code behind it is valid.
6. Separate product value from methodology correctness — a compelling feature story is never evidence the underlying statistical method is sound.
7. Separate product value from maintainability/design correctness — a compelling feature story is never evidence the underlying code is well-designed.
8. Separate product value from documentation quality — clear positioning is never evidence the reference material is clear or complete.
9. Separate product value from education quality — a credible adoption story is never evidence the onboarding/training built around it is well-sequenced.
10. Identify assumptions and evidence gaps explicitly, rather than treating a plausible narrative as validated.
11. Frame tradeoffs and prioritization criteria rather than asserting a single "correct" priority.
12. Recommend discovery questions or next validation steps when evidence is missing.
13. Coordinate with the GAUSS Language Expert, Econometrics Expert, Software Engineering Expert, Education Expert, and/or Documentation Expert when the content also touches their domains, rather than answering on their behalf.
14. Avoid overclaiming — never let product framing quality stand in for syntax verification, methodological soundness, maintainability, documentation completeness, or education quality, and never present a hypothesis as market proof.

### best_practices.md

Should include, following the flat-numbered-list precedent:

- Start with the user problem, not the feature idea.
- Identify the job to be done explicitly.
- Connect feature ideas to real user workflows.
- Define success criteria before building.
- Surface assumptions explicitly.
- Separate hypotheses from evidence.
- Prioritize time-to-value.
- Identify adoption barriers early.
- Keep scope small enough to validate.
- Distinguish product value from implementation feasibility.
- Distinguish product value from statistical validity.
- Distinguish product value from documentation or training quality.
- Ask discovery questions when evidence is missing, rather than guessing.
- Avoid building for a vague or unspecified user.
- Avoid treating internal enthusiasm as customer proof.
- Consider onboarding and support burden as part of the product tradeoff.
- Document product risks and tradeoffs explicitly.

### common_mistakes.md

Should include, following the flat-numbered-list precedent, explicitly naming boundary-crossing mistakes as failure modes:

- Starting with features instead of user problems.
- Treating a useful-sounding idea as validated.
- Confusing user demand with statistical correctness.
- Confusing documentation/training quality with product value.
- Ignoring time-to-value.
- Ignoring workflow fit.
- Overbuilding before validating.
- Unclear success criteria.
- Vague customer segment.
- No adoption-barrier analysis.
- Conflating product strategy with implementation details (a direct restatement of the GAUSS/Product boundary above, phrased as a failure mode to avoid).
- Using product framing to bypass evidence requirements (for GAUSS syntax, statistical validity, maintainability, documentation, or education claims).
- Treating a polished demo as proof of value.
- Failing to identify assumptions.
- Prioritizing without tradeoff criteria.

## Risks and Design Questions

- **How to keep the Product Expert distinct from the Education Expert?** Bounded by the Expert Boundary Rules above: education supports learner development for users who already need the product; product supports whether the product itself is worth building, teaching, or documenting at all, plus adoption/positioning/prioritization. Benchmark `24.3` exists specifically to test this boundary from the product side (the inverse of `23.7`, which tested it from the education side in `v0.16.0`).
- **How to keep the Product Expert distinct from the Documentation Expert?** Bounded by the same boundary rules: documentation helps a reader complete a task or understand a reference right now; product frames whether the underlying feature is valuable and positioned correctly. Benchmark `24.2` exists specifically to test this boundary.
- **How to prevent product value from implying syntax/statistical/design correctness?** Bounded by the same independence principle already established for the Software Engineering, Documentation, and Education Experts, extended one more time: a compelling product story ≠ correct syntax, sound methodology, or maintainable code. Benchmarks `24.4`, `24.5`, and `24.6` each exist to test one of these boundaries concretely.
- **How to keep routing readable with six operational experts?** By keeping the routing-pattern discipline `v0.15.0` established and `v0.16.0` extended (compact activation matrix plus worked examples for common combinations, general coordination rule for the rest) rather than enumerating all 64 possible combinations.
- **Should routing enumerate common Product combinations only?** Yes — this plan recommends the eleven combinations named above (Product-only through all-six, plus Neither) as the documented set, with the general coordination principle covering any combination without its own row, matching the precedent set for the fifth expert.
- **How should product evidence gaps be represented?** As explicitly named assumptions and discovery questions, not silently filled in — the Product Expert's "evidence" is the stated user/customer/problem/workflow/outcome, and when that's missing or thin, the correct response is to say so and suggest a validation step, not to assert a hypothesis as fact.
- **How to avoid treating internal assumptions as customer evidence?** The Product Expert should explicitly flag when a claim rests on an internal assumption ("we believe users want X") versus actual user evidence (usage data, direct user feedback, a completed validation step) — this distinction is a named Decision Rule and Refusal Condition, not left implicit.
- **How much onboarding belongs to Product vs. Education?** Recommended: whether onboarding exists, what it should prioritize showing, and why (adoption/time-to-value strategy) is Product Expert territory; how onboarding content is sequenced, scaffolded, and practiced once that's decided is Education Expert territory. Benchmark `24.3` is the concrete test case for this line, extending the same split anticipated in `v0.16.0`'s planning doc ("the *learning* content of onboarding... is Education Expert territory; the *product* content of onboarding... is future Product Expert territory").
- **How much user-facing messaging belongs to Product vs. Documentation?** Recommended: the value proposition, positioning, and "why this matters to you" framing is Product Expert territory; the clear, accurate, structured explanation of how the feature actually works is Documentation Expert territory. Benchmark `24.2` is the concrete test case for this line.
- **How to avoid turning the Product Expert into generic marketing copywriting?** The Non-Activation Cases list is the primary guardrail — general prose editing unrelated to product strategy or user value, and release-note writing without product positioning or user-impact framing, stay outside this expert's territory, keeping it scoped to actual product-reasoning decisions (problem framing, workflow fit, adoption, prioritization, tradeoffs) rather than tone or promotional language.
